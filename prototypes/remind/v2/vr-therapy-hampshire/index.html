What does VR therapy in Hampshire involve?

VR therapy in Hampshire at Re;mind is exposure therapy, the core of cognitive behavioural therapy for phobias. You practise in a headset rather than on a real bridge.

Avoidance brings relief in the moment, but it stops the fear from shrinking. Many people are simply tired of managing it. In the headset you can repeat a scene as often as you need, without leaving the room.

  • Amelia VR platformMore than 100 virtual environments, so there is a scene for many common fears.
  • In person in Whiteley[Assessments and reviews can also be by video.]

Does any of this sound familiar?

  • You park on the ground floor every time, even if that means circling twice.
  • At work you take the stairs rather than the glass lift.
  • Holidays get planned around the flight.
  • Or it is the crowd at the station, or the spider in the bath.

What happens in a VR therapy session at Re;mind?

Appointments last [50 minutes], with about [20 to 30 minutes] in the headset.

  1. 1

    Step 1: Assessment first

    We talk about the fear, your health and your goals, and check that a headset is safe for you.

  2. 2

    Step 2: Your ladder of scenes

    Together we list the situations you avoid, from easiest to hardest. You rate each from 0 to 100, and we match them to scenes.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Into the headset

    We start with a calm scene and move up only when your rating falls. The headset comes off whenever you want.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Talk, then practise

    Between scenes we compare what you expected with what actually happened, because that gap is where the learning sits. You leave with one real-life task for the week.

Does VR therapy actually work?

For specific phobias the evidence is good.

Read the studiesShow less

A meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials, 14 on specific phobias, found VR exposure clearly better than a waiting list. A second review showed the gains carry into real life. For heights, an automated UK trial with 100 adults found a large fall in fear that held at four weeks.

In short

  • About as effective as real-life exposure, and easier to start: three in four people chose VR when offered both.
  • Not a cure. Trials are often small with short follow-up, and results vary, so we measure rather than promise.
  • NICE has not issued guidance on VR for specific phobias: its VR assessment is about severe agoraphobic avoidance in psychosis.
  • The NHS phobias page lists talking therapies such as CBT among the main treatments.

Is VR therapy safe, and what are the side effects?

The main side effect is cybersickness: feeling warm, queasy or dizzy. It is most common in the first session and usually settles once the headset is off.

  • Short headset time, breaks, still scenes first.
  • The UK heights trial reported no adverse events among its 49 VR participants.
  • [Headset and controllers cleaned between every client; single-use face covers.]

Is VR therapy in Hampshire right for you?

If one specific fear is holding you back, probably yes; a few health conditions mean we talk first.

VR therapy suits you if you

  • Avoid a specific situation because of fear or panic
  • Have tried pushing through and found it too much
  • Would rather practise privately first
  • Have a clear goal, such as a lift or a bridge

Talk to us first if you

  • Have epilepsy or a history of seizures
  • Have a balance or inner-ear condition (not just dizziness at heights), or poor 3D vision
  • Get severe motion sickness, or are pregnant
  • [Are under 16]

What did VR therapy in Hampshire change for Claire?

Until January 2026, Claire, 41, from Locks Heath, had avoided heights for thirty years. She parked on the ground floor and waited at the foot of the Spinnaker Tower.

Seven appointments, 169 minutes in the headset: her Acrophobia Questionnaire score fell from 92 to 43 out of 160, and to 37 at three months.

At Easter she stood on the tower's glass floor, 100 metres up, knees shaking, and stayed. Glass floors still frighten her, but she now knows the fear passes.
