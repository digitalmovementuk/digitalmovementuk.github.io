What does VR therapy in Hampshire involve?

VR therapy in Hampshire at Re;mind is exposure therapy, the core of cognitive behavioural therapy for phobias. You practise in a headset rather than on a real bridge.

What happens in a VR therapy session at Re;mind?

Appointments last [50 minutes], with about [20 to 30 minutes] in the headset.

Easiest scene first; up a step only when your rating falls.

  1. 1

    Step 1: Assessment first

    First, a conversation about the fear, your health and your goals, as well as a check that a headset is safe for you.

  2. 2

    Step 2: Your ladder of scenes

    Together we list the situations you avoid, easiest to hardest, and rate each from 0 to 100, so that we can match them to scenes such as a glass lift.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Into the headset

    We start with a calm scene and move up only when your rating falls; the headset comes off the moment you want it to.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Talk, then practise

    Between scenes we compare what you expected with what actually happened, because that gap is where the learning sits. You leave with one real-life task for the week.

Does VR therapy actually work?

For specific phobias the evidence is good. A meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials, 14 on specific phobias, found VR exposure clearly better than a waiting list and about as effective as real-life exposure. A second review also showed the gains carry into real life. For heights, an automated UK trial with 100 adults found a large fall in fear that held at four weeks.

In short

  • About as effective as real-life exposure, and easier to start: three in four people chose VR when offered both.
  • Not a cure. Trials are often small with short follow-up, and results vary, so we measure rather than promise.
  • NICE has not issued guidance on VR for specific phobias: its VR assessment is about severe agoraphobic avoidance in psychosis.
  • The NHS phobias page lists talking therapies such as CBT among the main treatments.

Safety

Is VR therapy safe, and what are the side effects?

The main side effect is cybersickness: a warm, queasy or dizzy feeling, most often in the first session, which usually settles soon after the headset is off. The heights trial above reported no adverse events among its 49 VR participants.

  • Short headset time, breaks, still scenes first.
  • Pause or stop at any moment; the level never rises without your say-so.
  • [Headset and controllers cleaned between every client; single-use face covers.]
