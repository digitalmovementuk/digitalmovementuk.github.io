Does VR therapy for fear of heights work? It did for Claire

VR therapy for fear of heights can help, and Claire's story shows how. Over seven appointments at Re;mind, Claire, 41, from Locks Heath, tested her belief that her legs would give way. She went from a glass lift to a car park roof to a glass floor. Her fear scores roughly halved.

How did VR therapy for fear of heights give Claire her Saturdays back?

Claire came to Re;mind after cancelling a family trip for the third time. She had read about VR therapy for fear of heights and asked whether it could work after thirty years of avoidance. What tipped the balance was quieter, though: her nine-year-old daughter copying her caution, step for step, at a climbing-centre party.

  • Ground-floor parking onlySaturdays began with a hunt for a surface space in Fareham or at Gunwharf Quays.
  • The long way roundTen extra minutes on the school run rather than cross the footbridge.
  • At the bottom with the bagsEvery year the family went up the Spinnaker Tower while she waited below.

What did we measure first, and why?

The first appointment was mostly talking: the history, what Claire avoided and what she did to feel safe. Then we measured, because a fear this old deserves better than a guess.

  • Health: no seizures, balance or vision problems, or pregnancy, so a headset was safe for her.
  • Two short questionnaires: how she reads height situations, and how anxious and avoidant she feels in twenty everyday heights. Both scores were high, consistent with a specific phobia.
  • A mood check: nothing to treat first.
  • Her belief that her legs would give way and she would fall: 85 per cent.
  • Three goals, in her words: park anywhere, cross the bridge without thinking about it, go up the tower with the children.

What was the plan for VR therapy for fear of heights?

Test the fear in small, measured steps: in the headset first, then in real life the same week.

  1. 1

    Step 1: Understanding the fear

    When Claire gripped a rail and nothing happened, the rail got the credit, not her legs. Exposure lets the body learn instead.

  2. 2

    Step 2: A ladder of eight scenes

    Eight scenes from a first-floor balcony to a glass floor, each with an expected fear rating. We moved up only when the rating fell.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Graded exposure in the headset

    Five sessions of 20 to 30 minutes on the Amelia VR platform. Susie watched the same scene on a screen and asked for a rating every five minutes.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Real-life practice each week

    One agreed task a week, such as a glass-sided lift at a shopping centre, because practice makes the learning stick.

What happened in each of the seven appointments?

Ratings are out of 100. Each week was harder, so the peaks stayed high; the learning shows in the end ratings.

The Acrophobia Questionnaire rates twenty height situations for anxiety and avoidance; Claire's total fell from 92 to 43 out of 160, then to 37. The ten-step checklist is our own clinic tool, not a validated scale. In short: a large improvement, not a disappearance.

What happened on the glass floor at the Spinnaker Tower?

In week eight, Claire parked on the top deck of a Fareham car park for the first time she could remember, and did not look away.

At Easter, she bought four Spinnaker Tower tickets instead of three. She went up to the first viewing deck, 100 metres above the harbour. Then she stood on the glass floor for about thirty seconds while her daughter photographed their feet. Her fear peaked at 65, and she stayed.

She chose not to go up to the open-air top deck: a decision, not a failure, and one we had planned for.

At the three-month review she put it simply: "I still get the wobble on a glass floor. The difference is I know it passes. And I just park now."

What does Susie take from it?

What changed most is not how scared Claire felt but what she expected to happen. She tested her prediction again and again until the evidence outweighed it. A lift ride can happen ten times in twenty minutes; real life rarely allows that. Her belief fell faster than her fear, which is the pattern we look for.

What can VR therapy for fear of heights do, and not do?

VR therapy is one way of doing exposure therapy, the core of cognitive behavioural treatment for phobias. Results vary, so we measure rather than promise.

In trials it has worked about as well as facing the real situation. The gains also carry into everyday life (Carl and colleagues, 2019; Morina and colleagues, 2015; Freeman and colleagues, 2018).

Limits

  • It is not a cure and does not replace real-life practice.
  • It does not suit everyone, so we check your health first.
  • Facing real heights, step by step, works as well for many people; VR is simply an easier place to start.

What do people ask after reading Claire's story?

Often, yes, but we never promise. It depends on how strong the fear is, your health and what you want to be able to do.

Claire had seven; most courses fall between four and eight, agreed with you after the first appointment.

A few people feel warm or slightly queasy, as Claire did once; short sessions and breaks keep it manageable.
