A Re;mind case study

Does VR therapy for fear of heights work? It did for Claire

VR therapy for fear of heights can help, and Claire's story shows how. Over seven appointments at Re;mind, Claire, 41, from Locks Heath, tested the thought that her legs would give way: in a glass lift, on a car park roof and on a glass floor. Her fear scores roughly halved.

  • Seven appointments, 169 minutes in the headset
  • Fear scores roughly halved and held at three months
  • Her therapist beside her throughout

How did VR therapy for fear of heights give Claire her Saturdays back?

Claire came to Re;mind after cancelling a family trip for the third time. She had read about VR therapy for fear of heights and asked whether it could work after thirty years of avoidance.

She parked only on the ground floor, so Saturdays began with a hunt for a ground-level space. On the school run she walked ten extra minutes rather than cross the footbridge. Every year the family went up the Spinnaker Tower while she waited at the bottom with the bags.

What tipped the balance was quieter, though: her nine-year-old daughter copying her caution, step for step, at a climbing-centre party. Behind it all sat one thought: my legs will give way and I will fall.

What did we measure first, and why?

The first appointment was mostly talking: the history, what Claire avoided and what she did to feel safe. We also checked for anything that would make a headset unwise, such as seizures, balance or vision problems, or pregnancy; there was nothing.

Then we measured, because a fear this old deserves better than a guess. Two short questionnaires covered how she reads height situations and how anxious and avoidant she feels in twenty everyday heights. Both scores were high, consistent with a specific phobia; a mood check found nothing to treat first. She also rated her belief that her legs would give way at 85 per cent, and set three goals: park anywhere, cross the bridge, go up the tower with the children.

What was the plan for VR therapy for fear of heights?

  1. 1

    Step 1: Understanding the fear

    When Claire gripped a rail and nothing happened, the rail got the credit, not her legs. Exposure lets the body learn instead.

  2. 2

    Step 2: A ladder of eight scenes

    Eight scenes from a first-floor balcony to a glass floor, each with an expected fear rating. We moved up only when the rating fell.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Graded exposure in the headset

    Five sessions of 20 to 30 minutes on the Amelia VR platform, while Susie watched the same scene on a screen and asked for a rating every five minutes.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Real-life practice each week

    One agreed task a week, such as a glass-sided lift at a shopping centre, because practice makes the learning stick.

What happened in each of the seven appointments?

Fear ratings are out of 100: peak, then end.

  1. Week 0 · mid-January

    Assessment and a first look

    A first-floor balcony scene to check the headset suited her. Peak 35, then 20.

  2. Week 1

    Glass lift, floors one to five

    Hands off the rail from floor three. Peak 62, then 34. Queasy at minute 15, so a two-minute seated break.

Show all 7
  1. Week 2

    Floor eight and the open gallery

    Last week's floors rated 45, not 62. Then the open gallery, looking down for a full minute. Peak 70, then 32.

  2. Week 3

    Car park roof: the hardest session

    She froze on the first approach, then slow breathing and a fixed point on the horizon got her to a metre from the edge. Peak 78, then 41. That week's practice did not happen.

  3. Week 4

    Footbridge and ladder

    The car park edge rated 55 on return. Then a motorway footbridge mid-span and a three-metre ladder. Peak 72, then 33. Belief 50 per cent.

  4. Week 6 · after half-term

    Tenth-floor balcony and the glass floor

    A tenth-floor rail, looking straight down, then a glass floor: the course's highest peak, 80, then 36.

  5. Week 7 · early March

    Consolidation, in her order

    Lift to the top, car park edge, glass floor; the glass floor rated 58, down from 80. End 24, belief 25 per cent.

The Acrophobia Questionnaire rates twenty height situations for anxiety and avoidance; Claire's total fell from 92 to 43 out of 160, then to 37. The ten-step checklist is our own clinic tool, not a validated scale. In short: a large improvement, not a disappearance.

What happened on the glass floor at the Spinnaker Tower?

In week eight, Claire parked on the top deck of a Fareham car park for the first time in fifteen years, and did not look away.

At Easter, Claire bought four Spinnaker Tower tickets instead of three. On the first viewing deck, 100 metres up, she stood on the glass floor for about thirty seconds while her daughter photographed their feet. Her fear peaked at about 65, and she stayed anyway.

She chose not to go up to the open-air top deck: a decision, not a failure, and one we had planned for.

What does Susie take from it?

Susie Deacon

Founder of Re;mind; led all seven appointments in Whiteley.

What changed most is not how scared Claire felt but what she expected to happen. She tested her prediction, that her legs would give way, again and again until the evidence outweighed it. A lift ride can happen ten times in twenty minutes; real life rarely allows that.

VR therapy for fear of heights gave her a safe place to practise; the footbridge, the car park and the tower did the rest.

Please note

What can VR therapy for fear of heights do, and not do?

VR is one way of doing exposure therapy, the core of cognitive behavioural treatment for phobias. In trials it has worked about as well as facing the real situation, and the gains carry into everyday life (Carl and colleagues, 2019; Morina and colleagues, 2015; Freeman and colleagues, 2018). Results vary, so we measure rather than promise.

  • It is not a cure and does not replace real-life practice.
  • It may not suit people with photosensitive epilepsy, balance or binocular vision problems or severe motion sickness, or who are pregnant, so we check first.
  • Facing real heights, step by step, works as well for many people; VR is simply an easier place to start.

Questions people ask after reading Claire's story

Often, yes, but we never promise. It depends on how strong the fear is, your health and what you want to be able to do.

Claire had seven; most courses fall between four and eight, agreed with you after the first appointment.

A few people feel warm or slightly queasy, as Claire did once; short sessions and breaks keep it manageable.
